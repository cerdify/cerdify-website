10 things auditors and lenders ask for

Whether it's an audit, a loan renewal or a buyer's due diligence, the first requests are much the same. Here is what they usually are, and what a good answer looks like.

None of these is unusual, and most can be produced in minutes if they are kept each month. The trouble starts when they have to be rebuilt months later, from memory and email.

1. A trial balance and general ledger that agree

For the period under review, on the same accounting basis, with every account's ledger balance equal to the trial balance. Everything else is checked against these two reports. See how to tie them out.

2. Bank and credit card reconciliations

For every account, a reconciliation that agrees the book balance to the statement's ending balance, with the statement itself and a list of uncleared items. Old uncleared items will be asked about, so explain them or clear them.

3. An accounts receivable aging

One that totals to Accounts Receivable on the balance sheet, with old balances explained and a view on what will not be collected.

4. An accounts payable aging, and the bills that came after

The aging should total to Accounts Payable. Expect questions about bills received after the period end: any that relate to the period should already be in it.

5. A fixed-asset register

Each asset's cost, in-service date, method and life, with accumulated depreciation and the period's charge agreeing to the ledger. Invoices for the larger additions, and a note of anything sold or scrapped.

6. Loan statements and a debt schedule

Each loan's balance agreed to the lender's statement, payments split between principal and interest, and accrued interest recorded. Lenders will also check any covenants against the numbers.

7. Support for material transactions

Invoices, contracts, receipts and agreements for transactions above a materiality threshold. The document should be the right kind, cover the recorded amount and match the date, the vendor or customer and the account.

8. Payroll reports that agree to the books

The payroll provider's reports for the period, agreed to payroll expense and to the payroll liability accounts, with quarterly and annual filings available.

9. Schedules for accruals and prepaid expenses

For each accrual and prepaid balance, the calculation behind it and the document it rests on: the contract, the invoice or the policy. A balance with no schedule is a balance nobody can explain.

10. A list of journal entries, with who approved them

Manual journal entries get the closest look, especially large, round-number or period-end entries. Each one should have an explanation, support and a record of who approved it and when.

Keep it ready every month

The cheapest way to have all ten is to produce them as part of each month-end close rather than at year end. The month-end close checklist covers each of them.

Expect follow-up questions too, most often about large movements from the prior period. A one-line explanation for each, written at close, saves a long search later.
